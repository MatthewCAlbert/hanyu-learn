## Etymology
Ideographic. Shuowen's original sense is 骨閒肉肯肯著也, "flesh that clings
tightly to the bone" — describing meat that will not easily come free.
"To agree, to be willing" is a later, figurative extension: a decision that
sticks firmly, the way flesh sticks to bone. The top of the modern
character reads as 止 "stop," but that is a simplification-era shape, not
the 冒-derived component Shuowen analyses — best not read "stop" into the
meaning.

## Mnemonic
Meat 肉/⺼ clinging so tightly to the bone that it won't let go — a mind
made up and sticking to it.

## Notes
肯 in modern use is almost always the auxiliary "to be willing to" (肯不肯
"willing or not") or the intensifier in 肯定 "certainly, definitely" — the
literal "meat on bone" sense has fallen out of everyday use entirely.
