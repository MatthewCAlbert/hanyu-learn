## Etymology
**The famous "one line connecting heaven, earth, and man" reading is a Han-era
gloss, not the character's origin.** Shuowen records it and attributes it by
name to the scholar Dong Zhongshu — it is a philosophical explanation invented
centuries after the graph, reading meaning back into the finished shape.

Oracle-bone and bronze forms instead show a large, broad-bladed ceremonial
axe standing on its handle — the weapon that marked its holder as the person
with military and political authority. "King" is that authority's holder,
named after the axe he alone could wield. The three-horizontal-strokes shape
familiar today is a later, stylized flattening of the axe-blade outline.

## Mnemonic
A great axe standing upright, blade up: whoever holds it rules — 王, king.

## Notes
This is a textbook case of the playbook's warning about reverse-engineered
folk etymology: the "heaven-earth-man" story is genuinely ancient (Han
dynasty) and worth knowing as cultural history, but it explains the
character's later *interpretation*, not its Bronze Age *origin*.
